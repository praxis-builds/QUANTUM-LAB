# Lesson 15: Shor's algorithm for N = 21

## The idea
Same pipeline as Lesson 14: phase estimation of "multiply by a mod N", then continued fractions, the check a^r ≡ 1, and the gcds. Two things change.

First, the periods for N = 21 include 6 and 3, which are not powers of 2. The phases s/6 cannot be written exactly with t binary digits, so each peak spreads over neighbouring outcomes, as phase 1/3 did in Lesson 12. Continued fractions still find the right fraction from a nearby m.

Second, there is no tidy bit-rotation trick for multiplying mod 21. So each controlled multiplier is built generically: write down the full permutation of the 2⁵ work states (y → a·y mod 21 for y < 21, unchanged otherwise), turn it into a permutation matrix, and hand that matrix to the simulator.

## Predict first
1. How many qubits does N = 21 need with t = 2n counting qubits, compared with 12 for N = 15?
2. For a = 2 (r = 6), roughly what fraction of runs should return r? (Hint: the continued fraction gives s/r in lowest terms.)
3. Which values of a give the factors, and which fail?

## How to run

    .venv/bin/python lessons/15_shor_21.py

It prints four labelled steps (no plot) in a few seconds. Counting qubits are 0–9 and work qubits 10–14 (Qiskit order).

## What you should see, and why
Answers are below.

## Spoiler
1. 15 qubits: 5 work + 10 counting (3n in general), against 4 + 8 = 12 for N = 15. Each controlled multiplier now acts on 6 qubits, a 64 × 64 matrix instead of 32 × 32.
2. About one third. The outcome gives s/6 for a uniformly random s from 0 to 5, and only s = 1 and s = 5 reduce to a fraction with denominator 6 (2/6 = 1/3 and 4/6 = 2/3 give 3, 3/6 gives 2, 0 gives 1). The peaks also spread a little: m = 170 has 0.028 and m = 171 has 0.114, both giving r = 6. Exactly 0.322, which is slightly under 1/3 because a few outcomes land too far from any peak. Aer, over 2,000 seeded shots, gave 0.312–0.325 for the r = 6 values of a. With r = 2 or 3 the rate is 0.500 or 0.655.
3. a = 2, 8, 10, 11, 13 and 19 give 3 × 7. a = 4 and 16 have odd r = 3, and a = 5, 17 and 20 give a^(r/2) ≡ −1, exactly as Lesson 13 predicted without any quantum circuit. Over 500 seeded repeats of the full algorithm the factors were found every time: 198 by a lucky gcd, and otherwise after 1.85 quantum runs on average (at most 7).

**Honesty note.** Handing the simulator a 64 × 64 permutation matrix is compiled Shor at its most extreme: we computed every a·y mod 21 classically to build the "quantum" gate. A real device would need that multiplication as a circuit of elementary gates. General constructions such as Beauregard's (2003: 2n + 3 qubits, O(n³) gates) are far larger than anything here, and they must run without error. So the growth from 12 to 15 qubits understates the true cost.

**Classical baseline.** Trial division finds 3 on its second try. Lesson 13's brute-force period search needs at most 6 multiplications.

**What this does NOT show.** Any speed-up, or a quantum computer factoring an unknown number. The period, the multipliers and the answer were all available classically before the circuit ran.
