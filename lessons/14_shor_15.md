# Lesson 14: Shor's algorithm for N = 15

## The idea
Lesson 13 showed that factoring N only needs the period r of aˣ mod N. Shor finds r with **phase estimation** (Lesson 12). The gate is U = "multiply by a mod N", acting on a 4-qubit **work register** that starts at 1. Its eigenphases are s/r for s = 0 … r−1. So phase estimation with t counting qubits returns m ≈ (s/r)·2ᵗ for a random s.

**Continued fractions** (a classical method for finding the simplest fraction close to a number) turn m/2ᵗ back into s/r. The denominator is r whenever s and r share no factor. A cheap classical check, a^r ≡ 1 mod N, confirms it; otherwise run again. Counting qubit k needs U applied 2^k times. That is a single "multiply by a^(2^k) mod N", and a^(2^k) is computed classically by repeated squaring.

## Predict first
1. For a = 7 (r = 4) with 8 counting qubits, which outcomes m can appear, and with what probability?
2. What does the continued-fraction step return for each of them? What fraction of runs gives r?
3. Running the whole algorithm (random a, retry on failure), how many quantum runs does factoring 15 take on average?

## How to run

    .venv/bin/python lessons/14_shor_15.py

It prints five labelled steps (no plot) in about two seconds. The circuit has 12 qubits: counting qubits 0–7 and work qubits 8–11 (Qiskit order).

## What you should see, and why
Answers are below.

## Spoiler
1. m = 0, 64, 128 and 192, each with probability 0.250: these are (s/4)·256 for s = 0, 1, 2, 3. For N = 15 every period is a power of 2, so phase estimation is exact and nothing spreads (compare phase 1/3 in Lesson 12).
2. 64/256 = 1/4 and 192/256 = 3/4 give r = 4. 128/256 = 1/2 gives the candidate 2, which fails the check 7² ≡ 4, and 0 gives 1, which also fails. So half the runs give r: exactly 0.500, and Aer gave 0.507 over 2,000 shots. Every a gives 0.500 (Aer 0.495–0.512). Every a except 14 then yields 3 × 5; a = 14 fails as in Lesson 13.
3. Over 500 seeded repeats the factors were found every time. **240 of them needed no quantum run at all**, because a random a from 2 to 14 shares a factor with 15 in 6 out of 13 cases. Overall the mean was 0.72 quantum runs; when a quantum run was needed, 1.38 on average (at most 5).

**Honesty note: this is "compiled Shor".** Our multipliers were built from a permutation we worked out classically in advance: multiplying by 2 mod 15 rotates the 4 bits of y, and multiplying by −2^j adds a NOT on every bit. The powers a^(2^k) were precomputed too. To write those circuits you already need the answer's structure, which is fine for learning but is the well-known shortcut behind many "Shor demonstrations". (The NOT variants swap |0⟩ and |15⟩, states Shor never reaches from |1⟩; the script shows this.) A general circuit that multiplies by any a mod any N is much larger: Beauregard's construction uses 2n + 3 qubits and O(n³ log n) elementary gates in depth O(n³) (S. Beauregard, *Quantum Information and Computation* 3(2), 175–185, 2003; arXiv:quant-ph/0205095), against our 12 qubits and a few dozen gates here.

**Classical baseline.** Trial division factors 15 on its second try (2, then 3). Lesson 13's brute-force period search needs at most 4 multiplications.

**What this does NOT show.** A quantum computer factoring anything that was not already factored, or any speed-up: 15 is tiny, the multipliers were compiled from known answers, and the simulator ran on a classical computer.
