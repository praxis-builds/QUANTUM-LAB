# Lesson 26: BB84 key distribution

## The idea
Alice and Bob want a shared secret key. In **BB84** (Bennett and Brassard, 1984), Alice sends single qubits. For each one she picks a random bit and a random **basis**: Z (bit 0 → |0⟩, 1 → |1⟩) or X (0 → |+⟩, 1 → |−⟩). Bob measures each qubit in his own random basis. When the bases match, he reads Alice's bit exactly. When they differ, his result is a coin flip (Lesson 03).

Afterwards they announce their **bases** (never their bits) over a public channel and keep only the positions where the bases matched. This is **sifting**, and it leaves about half the qubits as a shared raw key. The fraction of positions where their kept bits disagree is the **QBER** (quantum bit error rate). It is the alarm Lesson 27 relies on: measuring a qubit in the wrong basis disturbs it.

## Predict first
1. What fraction of qubits survives sifting?
2. On a perfect channel, how many sifted bits disagree?
3. If the channel flips each qubit with probability p, what QBER do you expect?

## How to run

    .venv/bin/python lessons/26_bb84.py

It prints three labelled steps (no plot) in about two seconds. 20,000 qubits per run. Alice's and Bob's random choices come from a seeded NumPy generator, and every measurement from Aer (qubits of the same kind run as one seeded Aer job).

Answers are below.

## Spoiler
1. About half: 9,909 of 20,000 (0.4955). Bob guesses Alice's basis right half the time. The step-by-step table shows the other half: qubit 6 was sent as 0 in Z, measured in X, and came out 1.
2. None: QBER = 0.0000. Matching bases on a perfect channel always agree.
3. About p: 0.0098, 0.0314, 0.0527 and 0.0965 for p = 0.01, 0.03, 0.05 and 0.1, each within 1.2 standard deviations. "Flipped with probability p" here means an X, Y or Z error with probability p/2 each, which flips a qubit in either basis with probability exactly p. A channel that only applies X would give a QBER of p/2, because X does not change |+⟩ or |−⟩.

**Why this matters.** Alice and Bob cannot tell channel noise from an eavesdropper: both just produce errors. So every error must be paid for as if Eve caused it (Lesson 28), and a noisy channel costs key length.

**Classical baseline.** A classical signal can be copied perfectly and read without leaving a trace, so a classical scheme like this detects nothing. BB84's security rests on physics: an unknown quantum state cannot be copied (no-cloning), and measuring it in the wrong basis disturbs it.

**What this does NOT show.** Real photons: loss, detector noise, multi-photon pulses (Lesson 28's honesty notes). And the qubits here were never "sent" anywhere; this is a simulation of the statistics.
